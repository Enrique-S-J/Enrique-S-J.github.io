Case study

On-Prem LLM Platform

A private GPU inference platform serving 50+ daily users at a managed services provider (MSP), which eliminated thousands of dollars in monthly subscription and API overage spend.

01 Problem

AI usage was being paid for through subscriptions and metered APIs, and the subscription and API overage spend ran to thousands of dollars a month.

Cost was one of three reasons to act. The first was security: business documents and data were going out to cloud services when they could stay on hardware the company controls. The second was continuity: subscription plans cap usage, so people ran out of tokens partway through their work and had to stop or pay overages.

A private platform answers all three. It replaced paid seats that averaged about $100 per seat per month, which is $2,000 a month for a team of 20 before any extra token charges, and nobody on it runs out of usage.

02 Constraints

The platform is private and on-premise: inference runs on GPUs the company operates rather than on a metered third-party API, and it has to hold up for 50+ people using it every day.

The hardware is an NVIDIA DGX-1, the first AI-specific machine the company bought, and it was bought for this. It listed at about $150,000 when it was new and has 256 GB of GPU memory. That memory is the hard limit on everything else: which models fit, at what precision, and how many can be loaded at once.

It runs in the company's own data center, which is SOC 2 compliant, so prompts and documents stay on the company's own infrastructure.

03 Architecture

A LiteLLM gateway sits in front of the models and gives every client one OpenAI-compatible endpoint, so the model behind a request can change without any client knowing. Two front ends run against it — Open WebUI and LibreChat.

Running it is ongoing work rather than a one-time install: model deployment, quantization fixes, capacity monitoring, and version upgrades.

The front end is where people pick a model and work; the gateway is where access is controlled. Staff have keys with unlimited use: the hardware is already paid for, so the marginal cost of a request is electricity. Customers get the same gateway with either usage-based or key-based access, and the model size and GPU memory allocated to them scale with the size of their team and how much compute they want.

No model goes onto the production front end until it has been through limit testing on the hardware. Quantization is decided by constraints, not preference: what precision fits in the memory available, and whether a build at that precision exists for the model at all. Upgrades are done after hours. Anyone connected at the time sees a brief interruption, and a page refresh puts them back.

04 Decisions & tradeoffs

Over 20 models were benchmarked and 4 were standardized on.

The selection started on paper. I surveyed the open-weight models available at the time and built a model catalog of what the DGX-1 could actually run. For each model it records the memory footprint at full and reduced precision, which quantized builds exist and work on this hardware, token throughput, the use cases it suits (general chat, math, code, images and documents), and its license terms. The license column is there because the platform is also offered to customers, and some open-weight licenses do not allow paid hosting.

The binding constraint is GPU memory. 256 GB can hold many small models side by side or a few large ones, and the largest open-weight models do not fit usably at any precision. Every model that could run on the hardware was tested on it. Four is not the ceiling: it was enough for what the company needs, and it leaves memory to spare.

The tradeoff I would defend is quality for privacy and cost. Open-weight models on this hardware are, in my experience, about 90 percent as good as the commercial services for most everyday work, and the newest open-weight releases are in some cases just as good. In exchange, company data stays on company hardware, there is no per-token bill, and nobody hits a usage cap in the middle of a task. Privacy comes first: the risk of company data leaking is reason enough to run models privately. The cost is what makes it an easy decision.

05 Measured result

  • 50+ daily users on the platform.
  • Thousands of dollars in monthly spend eliminated from AI subscriptions and metered API overages.
  • 4 standardized models selected from 20+ benchmarked.
  • The platform seeded a private-compute offering.

Some people in the company have dropped their paid subscriptions and use the private platform instead. It did not take much selling: once someone sees what a private model can do, they usually want it. The customer offering grew the same way, out of consulting work, learning sessions, and people getting in touch with general questions about AI.
