Case study

On-Prem LLM Platform

A private GPU inference platform serving 50+ daily users at a managed services provider, replacing per-seat AI subscriptions with capacity the company owns.

01 Problem

Staff across the business had started using commercial AI assistants independently. That meant per-seat subscription spend and metered API overages growing month over month, with company and client information being pasted into tools the business did not control.

Edit me — the trigger

Replace this block with the specific moment this stopped being tolerable. Two or three sentences.

  • What surfaced the problem — a bill, an audit, a client question, someone pasting something they shouldn't have?
  • Who raised it, and who had to be convinced this was worth building rather than buying?
  • What was the alternative on the table (more seats? a policy banning the tools?) and why wasn't it enough?

02 Constraints

The platform had to serve a non-technical user base directly, which ruled out anything that required a terminal. It had to run on hardware the company owned, and it had to be worth the switch to people who were already happy with what they were using.

Edit me — the hard limits

Describe the constraints at the pattern level. No internal hostnames, no config values, no client names.

  • What hardware were you actually given, and was it bought for this or repurposed?
  • What was the budget and timeline, and did either change mid-project?
  • What compliance, data-handling, or client-contract requirements shaped the design?

03 Architecture

A LiteLLM gateway sits in front of the models and gives every client one OpenAI-compatible endpoint, so the model behind a request can change without anything downstream knowing. Two front ends run against it — Open WebUI and LibreChat — sharing the same gateway and the same model catalog.

Putting a gateway in the middle is the decision the rest of the platform rests on: it centralizes routing, keys, and usage accounting in one place, and it means swapping or adding a model is a configuration change rather than a migration.

Edit me — the shape of the system

Describe the topology at the level you'd use in a conference talk.

  • What serves the models themselves behind the gateway, and how do requests reach the GPUs?
  • How do users authenticate, and how is usage attributed back to a person or team?
  • Why two front ends rather than one — what does each do better?

04 Decisions & tradeoffs

Over 20 models were benchmarked and 4 were standardized on. Narrowing the catalog was a deliberate choice: a shorter list is cheaper to keep warm, easier to document, and easier for a non-technical user to choose from than a menu of twenty near-identical names.

Edit me — how you chose the four

  • What broke or surprised you during benchmarking? Which model did you expect to win and didn't?
  • What did the final four cover between them, and what did you give up by cutting the rest?

  • What did you deliberately not build, and has that held up?

05 Measured result

  • 50+ daily users on the platform.
  • Thousands of dollars in monthly spend eliminated from AI subscriptions and metered API overages.
  • 4 standardized models selected from 20+ benchmarked.
  • The platform seeded a private-compute offering — internal infrastructure that became something the business could sell.

Edit me — how you know it worked

Numbers are above. Add the texture behind them.

  • How fast did adoption happen, and did you have to sell it internally or did it spread on its own?
  • What do people use it for most — and was that what you expected when you built it?
  • How did the private-compute offering come about? Who asked for it first?
