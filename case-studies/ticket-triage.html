Case study

AI Ticket Intelligence

A retrieval-augmented triage engine over 30,000+ support tickets spanning 25 years, auto-routing incoming work to voice, network, and infrastructure queues and saving roughly an hour per ticket.

01 Problem

A managed services provider (MSP) had 25 years of support tickets, more than 30,000 of them. The project was to put that history to work on incoming tickets: retrieval over the archive, and automatic routing to the queue that owns the problem.

Edit me — the trigger

Replace this with what made triage the bottleneck worth attacking. Two or three sentences.

  • What did the triage process actually look like before — who did it, and how long did it take?
  • What was the cost of getting it wrong: a misrouted ticket, a repeated investigation, an SLA miss?
  • What made you believe the ticket history was usable, when most companies' archives are a graveyard?

02 Constraints

The automation works inside two guardrails: read-only by default, and human approval for writes. It can look things up and propose on its own; changing something takes a person.

Edit me — the hard limits

Pattern level only. No customer names, no internal hostnames, no ticket contents.

  • What shape was the data in, and how much cleaning did it need before it was usable?
  • What confidentiality rules applied to client data in tickets, and how did that constrain where the model could run?
  • Which actions count as writes here, and who approves them — what does that approval step look like in practice?

03 Architecture

Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) over the historical ticket corpus: an incoming ticket is matched against 30,000+ past tickets, and the retrieved history is the context the model works from. The output routes the ticket to one of three queues — voice, network, or infrastructure.

Edit me — how retrieval actually works

The specifics here are what separate this from a tutorial project.

  • How are tickets chunked and embedded, and what did you try before that didn't work?
  • What does retrieval run on, and why that — was it a real evaluation or a constraint?
  • How many neighbours are retrieved, and did you need reranking or metadata filtering to get quality up?

04 Decisions & tradeoffs

Routing has three targets: voice, network, and infrastructure. The automations are covered by test cases, so that when something fails they degrade safely.

Edit me — the decisions you'd defend

Pick two or three. Say what the obvious choice was and why you rejected it.

  • How do you handle a ticket the model isn't confident about — does a human see it, and what's the threshold?
  • Did you consider fine-tuning or plain classification instead of RAG? What decided it?
  • Why three queues rather than a finer taxonomy, and what happens to a ticket that is genuinely two problems at once?

Edit me — how you evaluated it

Anyone senior reading this will want to know how you measured accuracy.

  • What was your ground truth — historical routing, or human re-labelling?
  • Which categories got confused with each other, and what did you change as a result?
  • What do the test cases cover, and what does "degrade safely" mean concretely when the model or retrieval fails?

05 Measured result

  • Roughly one hour saved per ticket, adding up to hundreds of hours monthly.
  • 30,000+ tickets across 25 years put behind retrieval.
  • Automatic routing to voice, network, and infrastructure queues.

Edit me — where the saved hour goes

"An hour per ticket" invites a follow-up question. Answer it here.

  • Which part of the old process disappeared — the triage read, the search for prior art, the escalation?
  • How was the hour estimated, and would you defend that number in a room full of skeptics?
  • What did the engineers say once they'd used it for a month?
